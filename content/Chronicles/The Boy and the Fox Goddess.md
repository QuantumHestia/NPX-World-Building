# The Boy and the Fox Goddess

Under a canopy dense enough to turn midday green, fern and moss had swallowed all but a few stones of the old path. A small boy stumbled over the wet roots with his arms spread for balance, his laughter skipping between the trees with the patter of his bare feet.

“You’ll have to run faster than that. Or else I’m going to catch you.”

Her voice drifted from somewhere behind him, close enough that he ducked his head and ran harder, plunging through a curtain of silver leaves whilst branches tugged at his sleeves and mud squeezed between his toes. Every few steps he glanced over his shoulder, searching the green shadows for white robes or the sweep of a pale tail. The path behind him remained empty.

“Too slow,” she whispered beside his ear.

The boy shrieked as the fox goddess appeared where no path could have carried her, and his foot slid from a root when he tried to twist away. Before he struck the ground, eight white tails curled around his waist and shoulders, gathering him into a warm cocoon until his startled cry broke into breathless laughter.

She lifted him until their faces were level. Her slitted amber eyes narrowed, and one tail brushed a streak of mud from the end of his nose whilst another remained firm between his shoulder blades.

“Promise me you’ll take care of yourself,” she said. “Promise you won’t throw yourself at the first sign of danger.”

The boy pressed one muddy hand over his heart and drew himself upright within the tails. “I promise.”

A soft coo escaped her. She covered her mouth with one sleeve before setting him down, then drew out a cord and looped it around his neck. A wooden chrysanthemum settled against his chest, its eight petals polished smooth beneath her thumb.

“A reminder of that promise,” she said, and tapped one claw against the flower before releasing it.

---

“I did it,” the boy said.

Wind dragged through the empty field, worrying at the tall grass around his boots. Before him stood two gravestones, pale against the dark soil. He took a folded letter from his coat and laid it between them, weighting one corner with a pebble. Dragonkeep’s crest showed through the cracked green wax.

“Lord Ascelin signed it himself.” His thumb smoothed the parchment beside his father’s name. “You would have liked that.”

He was fourteen, nearing fifteen, and already held his shoulders stiff beneath his traveling coat. The wooden chrysanthemum rested beneath his collar, its cord worn rough where his fingers had worried it during the walk from the city.

He drew the flower into the open and wound its cord once around his knuckles. A shallow cut crossed one carved petal, catching against the pad of his thumb as Lord Ascelin’s letter rustled between the stones.

“Steel first,” he said. “Magic after.” His mouth tightened around whatever came next, and he lowered his head until the pendant pressed into his palm. “When I come home, I’ll know how to finish it.”

The valley wind turned cold. The pebble rolled from the letter, and the parchment lifted from the grass before a white tail settled across it, gentle enough to leave the cracked wax undisturbed. A bell sounded beyond the hills, followed by another farther away.

The boy followed the pale length of fur across the field.

The Fox Goddess stood beneath an alder tree, her white robes brushing the grass. Copper clasps held back her dark hair, and the other seven tails rested around her feet. She looked down at Dragonkeep’s crest.

“Lord Ascelin,” she mused.

“You.” The word scraped out between his teeth.

Her gaze went to the graves. The tails resting in the grass drew close around her ankles.

He pulled a narrow knife from his belt. Lord Ascelin’s crest had been stamped into the leather sheath, but the blade itself was cheap and newly sharpened.

“Say it,” he said.

She watched the point tremble between them. “Would you believe me?”

“Say it.”

Her eyes moved past him to the names carved into the stones.

He lunged whilst her attention rested on the graves.

A white tail caught his wrist and turned the blade aside. Another struck his knees, spilling him into the wet grass before either of them reached the graves. He twisted beneath the weight of her fur, cursing, and drove his free hand toward her face. She caught that wrist too.

“Fight me!”

The goddess knelt above him without answering. Her claws found the wooden chrysanthemum beneath his collar and pressed its carved center against his breastbone.

“All that practice,” the goddess said with a click of her tongue. “You still lead with your shoulder.”

“Why?” His shout tore at his throat. “Why did you do it?”

“Must there be a reason?”

“You murdered my parents in cold blood! Of course I want to know why!” Tears ran into the corners of his mouth. He pulled against the tails pinning his wrists until his shoulders shook. “I trusted you!”

The fox goddess held his gaze, then turned toward the two stones. One sleeve closed over her other hand.

“You will not trust my words.” Her claw pressed harder against the carved flower. “See for yourself.”

Heat entered the wood.

The field vanished.

He lay beneath his childhood table with his cheek against the floorboards. Smoke crawled along the ceiling. A hand gripped the back of his nightshirt, keeping him on his knees inside a circle cut through the kitchen floor.

His mother’s hand.

She looked younger than the woman beneath the gravestone, her hair bound away from a face filmed with sweat. A dark book lay open beside her. Its pages turned in the hot air, flashing diagrams of a flower with eight petals, each one pinned beneath an iron nail.

“Hold him still,” his father said.

He stood by the door with a long knife in one hand. Bone ash grayed his sleeves to the elbows. Beyond him, men hurried between the neighboring houses with oil jars cradled against their chests.

The child beneath the table began to cry. His mother tightened her grip.

“The eastern roofs are lit,” someone called from outside.

His father looked toward the orange light swelling beyond the shutters. “Then begin.”

The knife came down toward the wooden flower at the child’s throat.

Suddenly, white filled the doorway.

The fox goddess crossed the room without disturbing the circle. Her tails crowded the walls and ceiling, their tips blackened by the smoke outside. She looked at the blade, then at the hand knotted in the boy’s nightshirt.

“Let him go,” she said.

His mother pulled him against her legs. “The fire is in the circle.”

The cuts in the floor flared ember-red. Thin lines of light crept beneath his parents’ skin, and a burn across his father’s wrist sealed itself as the boy watched.

His father lifted the knife. “Hold his chin.”

The goddess glanced toward the window. A roof beam collapsed across the lane in a sheet of sparks. Someone screamed from the house beside it.

“The fires,” she said.

No one moved.

Foxfire swept through the room.

It passed over the child without heat and spilled beneath the walls, racing from house to house in pale streams. Outside, orange flames folded inward and died. Oil jars burst in the hands of the waiting men, drenching them as villagers poured into the lane.

His mother dragged the cord tight against his throat. His father rushed the goddess with the knife raised.

The memory broke into white fur, blood across the floorboards, and his mother’s fingers loosening one by one from his collar.

Then the field returned.

The boy rolled onto his side and retched into the grass. The goddess released his wrists and sat back on her heels. His knife lay several paces away, its blade dark with earth.

“A glamour,” he rasped.

She touched the pendant again. A narrow cut crossed the center of the chrysanthemum, almost hidden within the grain. His thumb settled into the groove, which years of handling had polished smooth.

“Wood remembers the blade,” she said.

He rubbed his throat. A faint ridge crossed the skin beneath his fingers. Fever, his mother had said whenever he asked about it. Now her arm locked around him again, firm and familiar, and his small hands clawed at the floorboards when the goddess came through the door.

“Why now?”

The goddess looked at the knife in the grass. “You came close enough.”

He followed her gaze. Mud clung to the crest on its sheath.

“I came to their graves.”

“You have done that before.”

On every earlier visit, he had shouted toward the tree line and fled before the bells could answer.

He tore the pendant over his head. The cord burned across the back of his neck. When he held the chrysanthemum toward her, the goddess tucked her hands into her sleeves.

“Take it.”

One of her tails curled around the knife and laid it at his feet.

“Choose what you carry,” she said.

The road divided beyond the graves. West, the grass gave way to alder shade and the first old stones of the forest path. East, beyond the hills, Dragonkeep’s towers caught the last light. Lord Ascelin’s letter pressed against the inside of his coat whenever he breathed.

He crouched and wiped the knife against his trouser leg. The blade slid home with a soft scrape. The pendant remained in his other hand.

“When I come back,” he said, “don’t hide behind your trees.”

The goddess’s eyes lowered to the flower. “I did not hide today.”

He knotted the broken cord and forced it over his head.

At dawn, he took the eastern road. He had almost reached the first hill when the forest bells began behind him. Their ringing thinned until the wind moving through the grass swallowed it.

---

Years later, the wooden chrysanthemum swung against the man’s breastplate as he pulled his sword upright.

He stood alone in a broken field, one armored hand braced against the pommel whilst the blade’s point sank deeper into the mud. Blood had dried between the plates of his gauntlet, some of it his own, and fresh red streaked the steel when he dragged the back of his wrist across his mouth.

Smoke had swallowed the first evening stars. Beneath it, an orange glow pulsed beyond the river where the village roofs had begun to collapse. The smell of scorched oil brought back a kitchen floor beneath his knees and his mother’s fist twisted into the collar of his nightshirt. His hand closed around the wooden flower until its petals pressed through the leather of his glove.

During his first winter at Dragonkeep, Lord Ascelin had placed a practice sword in his hands and asked whose guilt remained on the blade after a wrongful order. The boy answered that it belonged to the commander. Ascelin’s cane cracked across his knuckles, and the question came again. The cane lowered only when the boy answered, “Mine.”

At the far end of the field, mounted soldiers formed a line beneath a black-and-gold banner. Evening light flashed along their spearheads and helmet slits while the horses stamped at the trampled earth, blowing foam against their bits after the long pursuit.

Behind him, the forest rose in a wall of cedar and thorn. The last of forty villagers had vanished between those first trees, a woman bent beneath a sleeping child while two farmers carried the wounded miller ahead of her. Across the river, the bridge burned. Soot streaked every face that had turned toward him when he pointed to the forest.

“Run until the bells begin,” he had told them. “Then ask for sanctuary.” Sixteen winters had passed since the bells last answered him. The forest gave no answer now.

A rider detached from the line and crossed the field at a measured pace. The officer’s armor was lacquered red and spotless above dust-caked greaves; he stopped beyond sword reach, keeping one hand near the hilt at his hip.

“You have discharged your duty,” the officer called. “Stand aside.”

The man closed both hands around his sword’s pommel, shifting enough weight onto it to steady his left leg. “My duty was to protect them.”

“Your duty was to contain the village until the magistrate’s men arrived.”

Smoke stained the horizon behind the soldiers, flattening into a dark band where the river settlement had stood. By morning, the chimneys would be the tallest things left.

“I guess I misunderstood the order,” the man chuckled, and shifted his grip when the sword sank another inch into the softened earth.

The officer’s gaze moved over the broken shield near his boots, the blood along his side, and the empty field between one wounded man and fifty mounted soldiers. “Do you misunderstand the riders behind me?”

“I understand them perfectly.” His gaze passed along the waiting line before returning to the officer.

The officer drew the reins tighter. His horse tossed its head, metal rings clicking along the bridle. “The sickness must be contained.”

“They aren’t sick,” the man said.

The officer’s thumb stroked the leather wrapped around his reins. “They sheltered a forbidden spirit.”

“They left milk beneath a tree.”

“The distinction is recorded in the magistrate’s charge.” The officer stopped moving his thumb.

The man’s laugh caught against his ribs, and he pressed an elbow to his side until the pain loosened enough for him to breathe. “Then your magistrate can come explain it to me.”

The officer watched him for another moment, then wheeled his horse and raised one hand as he rode back toward the waiting line.

The man pulled his sword from the soil, set his feet, and lowered his weight over bent knees. Fifty riders. Spears long enough to reach him before his sword reached them. Archers waited behind the first rank, arrows already nocked. His left arm had begun to numb below the shoulder, and the forest bells remained silent.

The officer dropped his hand.

Hooves struck the earth in a gathering rhythm, flinging mud behind the first rank as the distance collapsed. The man watched the lead spear dip toward his chest, waited through the horse’s final stride, and moved beneath the point.

His blade knocked the spear aside before cutting across the rider’s thigh. As the rider fell, a second spear glanced from his shoulder plate and spun him halfway around; he caught its shaft beneath his arm, dragged the soldier from the saddle, and stepped into the space left by the passing horse. An arrow struck his breastplate and skipped away. Another grazed his cheek. The third entered beneath his raised arm.

His fingers opened around the sword.

The field pitched sideways, churned mud rising toward him as his knees gave way. Through the thunder of hooves came his own childhood voice beneath the trees: I promise.

Something soft caught him before he struck the ground.

Eight white tails swept across the field and curled around him, warm fur closing over the mud beneath his cheek. Arrows disappeared between the pale strands. The other tails tore furrows through the earth and swept the charging horses from their feet; the animals landed on their flanks and rolled clear, legs thrashing as their riders scattered.

When the man opened his eyes, the fox goddess stood between him and the broken charge. White robes stirred around her ankles, and the copper clasps in her dark hair had neither tarnished nor shifted since the day she caught him beneath the silver leaves. She looked over her shoulder at him, amber eyes wet and narrow, the tips of her claws pressed into her palms.

“You promised,” she hissed.

His head rested against one of her tails. Blood coated his tongue, but the corner of his mouth still lifted. “You took your time.”

“I was deciding whether to let you learn from the consequences.”

“And?”

“You have always been a slow student.”

Across the field, horses reared and dragged their riders through the mud whilst the officer in red shouted for the line to reform. His next command faltered when the goddess turned to face him.

She flicked one finger, and bells answered from deep within the forest.

Their ringing moved across the field in widening waves. Blood sank into the dark soil whilst roots tore free along the tree line and twisted together into an arch where no path had stood. The last villager stumbled beneath it with a child clutched against her shoulder; once she passed, the roots drew together and knitted the opening shut.

The goddess waited until the final thread of daylight vanished between them. “Those who entered my forest asked for sanctuary. They are under my protection.”

The officer drew his sword, though the men around him had fallen silent. “By decree of the provincial magistrate, the spirit inhabiting these woods is declared unlawful. Any shrine, offering, or act of worship made in its name is forbidden.”

Her gaze dropped briefly to the man cradled in her tails. “A forbidden spirit,” she murmured. “They do become inventive when they are frightened.”

“You will surrender yourself,” the officer continued, “and release the fugitives for judgment.”

One of her ears twitched. “No.”

The officer raised his sword another inch. “No?”

One corner of the goddess’s mouth lifted. His fingers curled into the fur beneath them; under silver leaves, that same smile had waited over her shoulder whenever her stride shortened and his bare feet gained on her.

“Pray tell, mortal. Do you believe you and your men face some helpless, forbidden spirit?”

The officer’s horse shied beneath him. He hauled on the reins until the bit cut foam from its mouth, then forced it back toward the line. “Archers.”

Bows rose behind him. Pale fire kindled at the tips of the goddess’s tails, each flame no larger than a candle, and with a loose sweep she sent them drifting over the field.

The soldiers loosed. Every arrow met foxfire and stopped, dozens of black shafts suspended beneath the evening sky whilst white flame traveled across their iron heads. Ash fell in their place, scattering over the grass as the foxfire continued toward the riders.

One flame settled upon a bowstring, another hovered between a horse’s ears, and others rested over armored shoulders close enough to cast blue-white reflections across the faces beneath. The flames gave off no heat. No one moved beneath them.

“Your courage belongs to numbers,” the goddess said. “Let us see how much remains when each of you stands alone.”

Moonless forest closed around each soldier. Their companions vanished, along with the banner and road, and roots crowded the strips of earth between pools of black water. Several dropped their weapons and called upon distant gods. Others hauled at their reins whilst their horses tore divots from the open field beneath them.

The officer remained before her, his horse trembling beneath his knees. Around him, his scattered company shouted at empty air.

“Release them,” he said.

“You came to my home with fire and iron.” One white tail lifted the fallen sword from the mud and carried it toward him. “You hunted frightened families to my boundary. Now you ask for mercy as though you remembered to bring some.”

The bloodied point stopped in the hollow of his throat.

The wounded man reached through the tails and caught the edge of her sleeve. “Don’t.”

Her ears angled back. “He shot you.”

“His archer shot me.”

“That distinction will comfort me for centuries.”

“Please.” His grip slipped along her sleeve before he caught it again. “Enough blood has been spilled.”

She looked down as he pulled the wooden chrysanthemum from beneath his breastplate. The cord had darkened with age, and one petal had split near its tip; mud filled the carved lines around its center. Her fingers opened slowly when she saw it.

“You told me to take care of myself,” he said, rubbing his thumb across the broken petal. “I’m trying to take care of what will remain when I’m gone.”

Her jaw shifted once. The sword stayed at the officer’s throat whilst the bells rang beyond the trees, then dropped into the grass beside his horse.

The moonless trunks vanished. Men cried out as the field returned around them, some sliding from their saddles whilst others turned their horses toward the road without waiting for an order. The foxfire stayed poised above their armor.

Three of the goddess’s tails lashed across the field, coiling around the officer’s neck and chest before lifting him from the saddle. His sword fell from his hand.

“You will return to your magistrate,” she said. “You will tell him the villagers crossed my boundary and received sanctuary. You will tell him every soldier spared here owes his life to the mercy of the man you condemned.”

The tails tightened when the officer tried to swallow. His boots hung inches above the earth.

“And if he sends us back?” he rasped.

“The fire will remember you.”

The flame above his shoulder sank through the lacquered armor. He clawed at the plates, but the foxfire left no scorch; a pale chrysanthemum bloomed across the red surface, held for the span of a breath, and faded beneath it.

“When you next cross this boundary with a weapon,” she said, “it will blossom.”

The officer looked toward his scattered company. His mouth opened, then closed.

The goddess released him. He struck the damp earth and scrambled for his horse, slipping twice before he seized the saddle and pulled himself up. He turned toward the road without another word, and the company followed, leaving bows and spears scattered across the field and the black-and-gold banner where its bearer had dropped it.

By the time the riders reached the distant hill, the goddess had carried the man beneath the trees.

---

He woke to crushed cedar and bitter herbs, their scents caught in the bandages wrapped beneath his shoulder and across his ribs.

The old shrine leaned over the clearing, smaller than it had been in his childhood, with moss softening the stone steps and paper charms stirring beneath the eaves. Ink-faded paper rasped whenever the night air passed through. Around the clearing, villagers slept in huddled groups beneath blankets woven from grass and leaves. Beside his mat, his armor lay stacked plate within plate, every buckle fastened.

The arrow was gone. Heat pulsed through the wound with each breath.

On the shrine steps, the fox goddess turned the wooden chrysanthemum between her fingers. “You broke it.”

His throat scraped when he answered. “I was shot.”

“The petal was cracked before today,” she said, holding it toward the nearest lantern.

“I fell from a horse in the northern pass.”

Her eyes lifted from the pendant and stayed on him. “You fell?”

“The horse objected to being stabbed.” At the narrowing of her gaze, he shifted carefully against the mat. “I objected as well.”

She returned to the pendant. Amber light traced the split petal beneath her thumb, filling the crack until the grain began to knit together. “You kept it.”

“You told me to.” His hand shifted across the blanket, reaching halfway toward the pendant before settling again.

“I didn’t,” she said, and the light faded from her fingers. “You also stopped visiting.”

He turned his face toward the dark beams beneath the shrine roof. Dust clung to the old carvings, and one of the bells above the eaves had split along its rim.

“Dragonkeep kept me through the winters. Then the army posted me farther away every year.” His hand closed around the edge of the blanket, pulling the weave taut across his stomach. “By the time I returned, the stones were sinking beneath weeds and strangers were sleeping under the roof.”

The chrysanthemum remained motionless between her fingers. “I thought you would be angry,” he said.

“I was.” She turned the flower over and ran one claw along the darkened cord.

“I thought you might have forgotten me.”

Her eight tails went still around the clearing. A paper charm whispered beneath the eaves, turned once on its cord, and settled.

She crossed the space between them and knelt beside his mat, pressing the chrysanthemum into his palm before folding his fingers over it. Her hand stayed over his for a moment, claws resting against the back of his knuckles.

“Mortals forget,” she said. “You made forgetting look so easy that I assumed it was another skill you had practiced.”

“I’m sorry.” His voice barely carried beyond the mat.

“You’re injured. I will postpone your punishment until you can survive it.”

“Generous,” he murmured, easing his shoulders back against the mat.

“I have been praised for my restraint.” The corner of her mouth lifted before a sleeping child murmured nearby, and she waited until the girl settled beneath her blanket before lowering her voice. “Where did you receive your orders?”

“At the provincial garrison. A sealed dispatch from the magistrate assigned my company to contain an outbreak along the river.”

“Did the dispatch name this village?” she asked, watching him over their joined hands.

“Yes.”

Her claw moved from his knuckle to the repaired petal. “Did it name the forest?”

Red ink enclosed the eastern boundary. A cross marked the old bridge for seizure, and a single road ran south from the river. At the map’s edge, the forest was a shaded mass without name or measure.

“No,” he said.

“Yet the soldiers carried a decree prepared for me.”

He pushed himself onto one elbow. The movement tightened the bandage around his ribs until white spots gathered at the edge of his sight. “They could have learned about you after reaching the village.”

“They stitched warding sigils beneath their banner’s crest, and their iron arrowheads had been quenched in salt. Those preparations began before they crossed the river.”

She opened his hand and touched one claw to the pendant’s central disk. Amber light spread through the wood, sinking beneath the carved petals until a second pattern surfaced in fine dark lines: the same angular seal woven beneath the soldiers’ crest.

His thumb drew back from it. “What is that?”

“A Hunter’s Mark.”

“It wasn’t there before.”

“Someone placed it beneath my blessing, where I could not see it until you crossed the boundary.” Her claws pressed into her opposite palm, dimpling the skin. “Someone handled this pendant whilst you slept or lay wounded. Someone who knew what it was.”

An armorer bent over his dented breastplate with both hands beneath the collar. In half a dozen infirmaries, healers cut blood-stiffened straps from his shoulder whilst the pendant lay exposed against his throat.

Outside the clearing, the shrine bells began to ring, although the paper charms hung motionless beneath the eaves.

The goddess turned toward the dark path leading back to the boundary.

“The villagers were bait,” the man said.

“So were the soldiers.”

He closed his hand around the chrysanthemum. Its repaired petal pressed into the base of his thumb, warm from her touch, whilst the hidden seal remained dark beneath the grain.

When the goddess looked at him, her mouth had gone slack and one hand had risen to the hollow beneath her ribs. Her teeth had flashed beneath the trees, and her tails had swayed whilst a muddy child tried to outrun her. Now even their pale tips rested motionless against the roots.

“You were never sent here to contain an outbreak,” she said. “You were sent here to make me come out.”

For a moment, he heard only the bells and the unsteady breathing of the villagers asleep beneath the eaves.

“Then they know this place,” he said.

“They know enough.” The goddess took the pendant from his hand before he could close his fingers around it. The wooden petals had gone cold. “The mark answered when I opened the boundary. Whoever laid it beneath my blessing will know that I sheltered you, and that I did so here.”

He watched her thumb move over the central disk, stopping just short of the dark seal. “Can you remove it?”

“I can break the pendant.”

His eyes rose to hers.

Her thumb stopped against the repaired petal. “It would sever the mark from you. It would also destroy what remains of my blessing.”

His fingers closed around the pendant until the repaired petal pressed into his palm. For years it had struck against his breastbone with every marching step. It had lain cold against his skin beside his parents’ graves and warm beneath his armor after battle, even through the years when he could not touch it without seeing her hand at his throat. His thumb covered the flower’s center. The breath beneath his bandages stopped halfway in.

“Do it,” he said.

The goddess’s gaze stayed on him. “You do not have to prove anything to me by surrendering what you love.”

He looked past her toward the sleeping families gathered among the roots. “They cannot carry this for me.”

Her fingers closed around the chrysanthemum. Amber light reached the edges of its petals; her thumb covered the dark seal, and the light went out.

“No,” she said.

He frowned. “No?”

“The mark has already done its work. Breaking the pendant would leave you without its protection and tell our hunters exactly when we discovered it.” She returned it to his palm and folded his fingers over the wood. “We will give them another answer.”

Beyond the clearing, something answered the shrine bells: a single dull chime from the far side of the trees.

The goddess rose. Her tails drew close around her legs and held there whilst the paper charms beneath the eaves began to turn in the still air. “Wake the villagers,” she said. “Tell them to take only what they can carry without a lantern.”

He pushed himself upright too quickly. Pain flared through his side, and the shrine floor lurched beneath him. One of her tails caught his shoulder before he struck the ground.

“You are staying here,” she said.

“I know their routes.” He pressed a hand to the fresh bandage. “The bridge is gone, and the road north is likely watched. There is an old charcoal track west of the miller’s field. It joins the ridge road before it reaches the river.”

“You learned this whilst leading forty frightened people through my forest?”

“I learned it whilst trying to keep them alive.”

Her ears angled back. The tail at his shoulder stayed there through another breath before sliding away. “You will walk beside me. If you fall, I will carry you. If you argue, I will carry you by the ankle.”

“That seems inefficient.”

“It is memorable.”

He used the shrine post to steady himself and moved among the sleeping villagers. The first people he woke stared at his bloodstained bandage and the fox goddess waiting among the roots, then passed the warning from blanket to blanket. No one screamed. A mother tied a sleeping child to her back whilst a girl searched beneath the leaves for one missing shoe.

At the edge of the clearing, the goddess lifted one hand. The roots parted soundlessly, exposing a narrow path that ran west beneath cedar boughs. Darkness closed over it after a few steps.

“The forest will hide you until dawn,” she told them. “After dawn, you will follow his instructions.”

The miller, his arm in a sling, looked from her to the man. “And you?”

The goddess glanced toward the unseen boundary, where the distant bell rang again.

“I have guests coming,” she said.

The pendant warmed against his palm.
